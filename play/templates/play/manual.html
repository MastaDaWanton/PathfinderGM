A Pathfinder 1st Edition game you play like a story. A language model on your own machine narrates and plays everyone who is not you; a rules engine owns every number, every condition and every die. Nothing leaves your computer.

What you need

Windows, and Ollama. Ollama is the program that runs the language model. It is free, it installs into your own account without asking for an administrator, and this app does not bundle it — partly because the models are far too large to ship inside an installer, and partly because if you already have Ollama you should keep using the one you have.

Then the models themselves. The app fetches these for you; you never type a command.

Will it run well on my machine?

The narrator is a 12-billion-parameter model. It wants roughly 8 GB of video memory to be comfortable. With less than that Ollama will fall back to your processor and main memory, which works and is slow — minutes per turn rather than seconds. You will also want about {{ disk_wanted }} free on whichever drive Ollama keeps its models on.

How fast is a turn, honestly? On a machine with a recent graphics card, roughly fifteen seconds — sometimes forty when the turn is complicated. The first turn after you start the app is slower, often close to a minute, because the model has to be read into memory once; after that it stays loaded.

This is the price of the whole thing running locally, with no account, no subscription and nothing sent anywhere. It is a real cost and it was chosen deliberately. Most of a turn involves no model at all — every roll, every modifier and every condition is decided by code, instantly — so the wait is the writing, not the rules.

If you would rather not run a model locally, Settings lets you point any of the four jobs at a hosted provider with an API key instead. Local is the default and always will be; it is not compulsory.

The first run

  1. Install Ollama and start it.
  2. Open Pathfinder GM and go to Settings. The top section says what is missing.
  3. Press Download beside the model it names, and wait. There is a progress bar; you can leave the page open and come back.
  4. When it finishes, the warning disappears and you can start a game.

Until that is done the app will not open the table — it would only be able to show you an error where the story should be. Everything that does not need a model stays open the whole time: making a character, the crafting benches, and all the homebrew editors.

Worlds

This app does not invent the world. It plays inside one written by World Bible, a separate program that generates a complete setting — its peoples, its towns, its factions, its history — and exports it as a single file.

{{ shipped_worlds }} {{ shipped_verb }} with the app, so you can play straight away. To add your own, export one from World Bible and press Import a world on the shelf. The file is read and checked as it lands, so a world this build cannot play is refused there and then rather than halfway through a session.

The world file is never written to. Everything your game changes — a dead merchant, a burned bridge, a warrant out for your arrest — lives in your campaign save instead, so the same world can host several campaigns and re-exporting it never damages one.

Your character

Full 1st Edition character creation, with the rules enforced as you go: {{ classes }} classes, {{ races }} races, {{ feats }} feats and a point-buy spread that the app checks rather than trusts. Press Create a character.

Or take one of the ready-made characters on the shelf if you would rather start playing than start building.

Where you were before

You may also pick a background: {{ backgrounds }} backgrounds ship, in groups like the trade, the road and the law. A background is not a personality quiz — it names somebody in this world who already knows you. Pick Apprenticed and, once your world is chosen, the game decides which trader you served under and where, and says so in the first paragraph you read.

Backgrounds grant standing, not power: at most two skill points, in a bonus type of their own so they never stack into something a feat would charge for. What they really give you is a door you can walk through without explaining yourself. It is optional — a character with no past is a perfectly ordinary way to arrive.

After the build comes outfitting: your starting gold, spent on real equipment at real prices, before anybody opens a door. You level up from the sheet when you have the experience, all the way to 20th.

You play one character. Everyone else in the world — allies, hirelings, enemies, the person behind the counter — belongs to the GM. The dice popup only ever asks you for your own rolls.

Playing

Type what you do, in your own words, into the box at the bottom. "I ask the stallholder who owns the mill." "I follow the road north." "I look behind the curtain." The GM answers in prose and the engine resolves anything mechanical inside it.

Above the box there are usually two or three suggestions — things you could plausibly do next. They are a shortcut, never a menu: anything you can type is allowed, and the suggestions are only there so you are never staring at an empty box.

The dice

When something you did needs a roll, a die appears and you roll it. Every modifier that went into the total is itemised beside it, so you can always see why the number is the number. Rolls that are not yours — an enemy's attack, a guard's Perception, a hidden check — are made by the engine and never shown to the GM before they happen.

This is the arrangement the whole app is built around: the GM proposes and the engine disposes. The narrator can say a blow is struck; only the rules decide whether it landed and for how much. It cannot talk the game out of its own rules, because it is never asked.

Things that start without you

The world has {{ quests }} quest lines in it, and none of them waits to be accepted. A debt in a ledger, a sickness in a village, something taking the herds: each one looks for a place and a person that suit it, and opens where it fits — so the trader who owes money is a trader this world actually has, standing where the world put them.

You are never told that a quest has begun, because nobody in the world would say so. You hear about it the way you would hear about anything: somebody mentions it, a letter finds you, a crier says it in the square. Walking away is an ending like any other, and the world goes on having the problem.

Leaving town

You are not stuck in one place. Where the world has written a road between two settlements you can take it, and it costs days — the clock moves, you camp on the way, and whatever was happening behind you carries on happening. Anyone who is not coming is left where they stood.

How long a road takes depends on how fast you walk and what you are walking over. If the world said how far it is, the rules do the arithmetic; if it did not, the game tells you how many days it took and does not invent a distance nobody wrote down. Some settlements have no road written to them at all, and it will say so rather than pretending.

Time, food and rest

Your sheet tracks thirst, hunger and rest, and the world's clock runs whether you are watching or not. Resting, eating and travelling all take real time, and the people around you get on with their own business in it.

Fights

When a fight starts you get initiative, a turn order, and a panel of what you can actually do this turn: Strike, Full attack, Ability, Cast, a swift action, a free action. Pick a target and commit the turn.

Some scenes also carry a map — a five-foot grid you can move on by clicking a square. Where there is one, reach, cover, charge lanes and attacks of opportunity are measured from real distance rather than asserted. Where there is not, the same three ranges — engaged, near, far — are tracked without one.

Up and down

A room is not flat. Most places are built with something to climb — a gallery, a landing over the hall, the wall-walk above a gate, ground that rises — and the map has a level to look at, so you can see who is up there and stand on it yourself. Somebody on another floor of the same room is faded rather than hidden; they are still there.

Height is worth something. Swinging down at somebody is worth +1, though shooting down at them is worth nothing — that is the rule, not an oversight. A rail along a gallery is cover for both of you and stops neither. A thing with a climb speed, like a spider, is dangerous on a wall in a way a person climbing one is not, and a flier indoors only has as much air as the ceiling leaves it. You cannot get up somewhere without a way up, and the game will say which you are missing.

Buildings have floors, reached by their stairs and only by their stairs. A fireball is a sphere — it has a top, so a flier high above one is genuinely out of it.

Conditions, buffs, durations, iterative attacks, manoeuvres, damage reduction, resistances and stacking rules are the engine's business and are applied whether or not anybody mentions them. {{ spells }} spells and {{ creatures }} creature stat blocks are loaded and available to it.

Asking questions, and cheating

Two things you can type that are not your character speaking. Both start with a slash, so no ordinary sentence can be mistaken for one.

What you typeWhat happens
/gm who runs this town? Answered out of character, from the world's own material and the state of your game — not made up. Answers read off the engine or the books are labelled differently from answers the model had to reason its way to, so you can always tell a fact from an opinion.
/cheat I have 1000 gold You become the author for one line. Use it to fix something that went wrong, or to skip past something you do not want to play out.

Neither goes into the story. The narrator never sees the question or the answer, so it cannot quietly turn one into a fact about the world on the next turn.

Crafting. {{ disciplines }} — each with its own materials, recipes, and the gathering that feeds it. Reachable from the shelf or from the table.

When something goes wrong

What you seeWhat it means
A warning on the front page about Ollama Exactly what it says. Open Settings, where the fix is a button.
"The GM could not produce a turn" The model failed every attempt at a valid turn and the app told you rather than inventing one. Try again, or rephrase what you typed. Roughly one turn in twenty needs a retry; failing outright is rarer.
A turn is taking minutes Either the first turn after opening the app, or the model is running on your processor rather than your graphics card.
The GM refuses something you tried Usually correct. You cannot use an ability your character does not have, or change somebody's mind by announcing that you have. Persuading, threatening, lying and bribing are all ordinary play and do work — they are skill checks.
